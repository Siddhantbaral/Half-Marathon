Day one: a heart rate I couldn’t control

I tried to stay in zone 2 for the whole first run, but after 2.5 km my heart rate jumped to zone 4–5 and stayed there. I wrote down a hope, and the rest of this article checks it.

“I hope this improves with time and I can run 6:30-7:00 /km in zone 2.”26 Apr, Kirtipur Easy, Day 1

The build: 41 km in the first full week, 65 at the peak

Mileage climbed in steps and dipped every time something went wrong. In mid-May both shins gave out, and later a fever and an illness cost me weeks. My peak was 65 km in the week of 7 September. The last long run before taper was 17.3 km on 20 September.

8 June: the day I fixed my zones

Garmin’s default zones use only age and max heart rate. On 8 June, Day 44, I rebuilt them with the Karvonen method, which also uses my resting heart rate. With the default zones, zone 2 ended at 142 bpm. In my own zones, 142 is where zone 2 starts and it runs up to 156. The new setting also changed how I ran: I built separate recovery, speed, tempo and long-run workouts, and the watch buzzed when I crossed my limit, so I looked at it less and ran by feel. Four days later, at the Morning Beats 5k, my watch recorded a max heart rate of 203.

“This uses both highest and resting heart rate to compute zones.” … “Garmin default only uses age and max HR.”8 Jun, Kirtipur Recovery Run, Day 44

Before the switch my easy runs averaged 143 bpm at 7:22/km (24 runs). After it they averaged 145 bpm at 6:43/km (38 runs). My heart rate went up only about 2 bpm, but I got 39 seconds per km faster. Fitness and zones changed in the same weeks, so I can’t separate the two, but my own zones let my easy runs sit where my heart rate could actually take them.

“These zones were setup when my resting HR was 51, now its 47-48”27 Aug, Kirtipur Easy Z2, Day 101

My easy runs got a minute per km faster

To track progress fairly, I looked only at the stretches of easy runs where my heart rate sat between 140 and 155 bpm. In May, that cost 7:03/km. In September and October it cost 6:05/km, about 57 seconds per km faster at the same heart rate. That beats the 6:30–7:00 I hoped for on day one. The trend flattened in June, when I switched to Karvonen zones and then got sick.

“I made my heart rate zones according to karvonen method.” … “This run really felt easy.”8 Jun, Kirtipur Recovery Run, Day 44

The 400s: from going out too fast to nailing it

Intervals were where I was most honest with myself. 66.0 of my 109 logged reps landed inside 4:00–4:18/km. Session averages went from 4:16 in the first two sessions to 3:56 in the latest three. The fastest rep was 3:44/km, though some of the late reps used a downhill stretch.

“I need to learn how to pace a 400m. I go out too fast and by the 200m mark I am almost done.”16 Jun, Kirtipur Speed Interval Run, Day 52
“I think I executed this perfectly.”7 Jul, Kirtipur Speed Interval Run, Day 69

Long runs: 10 km to 23.3 km, and the finish got faster

I ran 22 long runs. Distance more than doubled, but the bigger change was how I ran them. In June each long run was one steady pace. From late July I ran the first part easy and finished faster: 6 km at 5:32/km on 2 August, 7 km at 5:03 on 23 August and 8.6 km at 5:16 on 6 September. The easy part got quicker too, from 6:42/km in June to 6:16/km in August. Two of these runs were on zero sleep.

Where my heart rate lived

With the zones fixed, my time in zone 2 (142–156 bpm) went from 36% of my running time in May to 59% in September. Time above 156 bpm roughly halved, from 35% to 18%. October looks different on purpose: taper.

Race day: a negative split and a hill-shaped pace line

The first half took 0:54:27 and the second took 0:52:04, a negative split of 2:23. My slowest full kilometre was km 2 at 5:37 and my fastest was km 17 at 4:35. Pace followed the terrain closely: across the 21 full kilometres the correlation between net climb and pace was 0.79. My heart rate averaged 184 bpm and peaked at 198.

Did my notes match the numbers?

Partly. I scored each note for upbeat and negative words and compared it with how high my heart rate was relative to what my pace, terrain and fitness on that date predicted. Across 41 easy and long runs the correlation was -0.26: upbeat notes tend to come from runs where my heart rate was lower than expected, but the link is weak. I blamed a lot of bad runs on sleep. Across the 10 runs where I wrote down the hours, the correlation was -0.30. My two zero-sleep long runs went opposite ways: one came in below my expected heart rate and the other well above.

“I am a competitive person and it was faster than this.”20 Sep, Kirtipur Long Run, Day 118 (about the 5:20 target)
